Reality, Space, and AI:
From Truth to Patterns — and Back Again

A reflection on GeoAI, spatial intelligence, and the need to reconnect data-driven inference with geographic structure, knowledge, causality, and human responsibility.
Marinos Kavouras · Professor Emeritus · Reflections following the AI for Good Global Summit, Geneva · 2026

For centuries, science has pursued truth through explanatory models. We developed theories, classifications, maps, and structured knowledge systems not only to describe what happens, but to understand why. Artificial intelligence has introduced a powerful complementary paradigm: rather than specifying every mechanism in advance, it can infer remarkably complex regularities directly from data.

This shift is profound. But it also creates a question that becomes especially important when AI is used to understand and act upon the real world:

Are we moving closer to understanding reality — or simply becoming exceptionally good at detecting patterns?

1. From explanation to correlation

The data revolution has changed the balance between explicit knowledge and statistical inference. Satellites, sensors, mobile devices, administrative systems and digital platforms continuously generate observations of the Earth. Deep learning can discover relationships at a scale and dimensionality that exceed unaided human perception.

For prediction, this is extraordinarily valuable. Yet prediction and explanation are not the same epistemic task. A model may forecast flooding, crop stress or urban change with high accuracy without identifying the mechanisms that generated the phenomenon. A pattern can therefore be useful without being a complete account of reality.

Figure 1 — The productive loop is not data → prediction alone. It connects observation, pattern discovery, knowledge, decision and the return to the ground.

2. The danger of letting the pattern become the reality

A pattern is a regularity observed in data. Truth, in the stronger scientific sense, requires interpretation, context, explanatory adequacy and, where appropriate, causal justification. Correlation is not causation; frequency is not meaning.

The danger becomes greater when predictions are used to intervene in the world. Decisions based on historical data can change behaviour, institutions and places, thereby changing the very data from which future models learn. The system can begin to reinforce its own assumptions.

The old Pirandellian question — Così è, se vi pare — acquires a striking technological resonance: what happens when the statistical representation of reality becomes the reality against which subsequent decisions are made?

3. Geography is the stress test

This is where geography matters. Geography is not merely another application domain for AI; it is a particularly demanding test of whether an AI system can move beyond generic statistical regularity toward context-sensitive understanding.

Many machine-learning workflows are developed under assumptions of independent and identically distributed observations, or are evaluated using validation procedures that implicitly rely on those assumptions. Spatial data frequently violate them. Nearby observations can be dependent; relationships can vary from place to place; and the meaning of a variable can change with scale and context.

Space is therefore not simply another column in a database. It is a structure.

Figure 2 — Geographic dependence, heterogeneity and scale challenge the idea that observations can always be treated as interchangeable rows in a table.

Modern GeoAI already addresses these issues through spatial representation learning, heterogeneous models, geospatial foundation models and other explicitly spatial methods. The point is not that neural models cannot learn spatial regularities. They can. The deeper question is whether the learned representation preserves the geographic structure, semantics and mechanisms needed when a model is transferred across places, scales or decision contexts.

A European urban pattern may be highly predictive within its training environment and still fail in an informal settlement elsewhere. A model can recognize the geometry of a boundary without knowing its institutional meaning. A river is not merely a polyline; a city is not merely a distribution of pixels; a place is not exhausted by its coordinates.

4. From GeoAI to Spatial Intelligence

The next step is not to reject data-driven AI. It is to complement it.

Neurosymbolic and knowledge-guided approaches offer one route toward this synthesis: neural systems can detect and represent complex patterns, while symbolic or knowledge-based components can provide explicit semantics, constraints, rules, ontologies, knowledge graphs and reasoning. Causal models can add another essential layer when the question is not simply what is likely to happen? but what might happen if we intervene?

Figure 3 — A conceptual Spatial Intelligence architecture: learned perception is coupled to explicit geographic knowledge, reasoning and decision support. This is a design vision, not a claim that one standard architecture already exists.

The important word here is grounding. A hybrid system should not be considered trustworthy merely because it contains a symbolic module. Nor does neurosymbolic design automatically guarantee explanation, causality, generalization or lower energy consumption. Those are properties that must be demonstrated for the particular architecture and task.

But the direction is compelling: move from systems that merely recognize patterns toward systems that can relate patterns to place, meaning, constraints and possible interventions.

5. Spatial Decision Support: the real destination

This matters because the ultimate value of GeoAI is not prediction for its own sake. It is better understanding and better decisions about the world.

Better decisionsContext-sensitive recommendations that respect local conditions rather than relying only on global statistical averages.
Better governanceModels whose assumptions, evidence and constraints can be inspected, challenged and discussed.
Better understanding of placeRecognition that communities, ecosystems and cities are evolving spatial systems, not static records.

For Spatial Decision Support, the progression therefore becomes:

  • Where? — spatial observation and representation.
  • What pattern? — data-driven inference.
  • Why here? — geographic knowledge, context and causal reasoning.
  • What if? — intervention, scenario and counterfactual analysis.
  • What should we do? — accountable decision support.

This is where GeoAI can become more than an application of AI to maps. It can become a foundation for a broader Spatial Intelligence: intelligence that is explicitly grounded in place, relationships, scale, semantics, dynamics and human decisions.

6. An unbroken loop

The future is neither a nostalgic return to purely traditional knowledge systems nor a blind surrender to ever-larger data-driven models. It is a synthesis.

Data reveal patterns. Geographic knowledge tests and interprets those patterns. Causal reasoning asks what can legitimately be inferred about interventions. Human institutions and decision-makers determine what should be done. The resulting decisions act upon the ground — and the ground generates new observations.

GeoAI reaches its full potential not when it replaces knowledge with data, but when it reconnects data with knowledge, place, causality and responsibility.

That is the journey from truth to patterns — and back again.

And perhaps this is the deeper proposition for the next generation of GeoAI: intelligence without spatial understanding is incomplete.
